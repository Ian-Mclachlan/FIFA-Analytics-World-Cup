User Guide — How to Use This Dashboard

Overview

This dashboard provides probabilistic medical surge forecasting for mass gathering events. It runs six validated and evidence-based models simultaneously, generating patient volume estimates, transport projections, hospital load scores, and confidence intervals — all updated in real time as you adjust inputs.

Step-by-Step Instructions

  • Open Parameters: Click "▼ Parameters" in the header bar to reveal the input panel. This collapses to keep the command view clean once configured.
  • Set Event Parameters: Enter the expected attendance, forecasted temperature (°F), humidity (%), and kickoff hour (24h format). Select venue type, seating configuration, environment, event type, and duration from the dropdowns.
  • Set UVa Threat Modifiers: Select the expected spectator age profile, anticipated crowd mood, and alcohol availability level. These feed the UVa categorical threat model only — they do not affect other models.
  • Set Hospital Baseline (NEDOCS): Enter the current state of the nearest receiving Emergency Department: census, bed count, admitted boarders, total hospital beds, ventilators in use, longest admit wait time, and time since the last bed was assigned. These inputs feed the NEDOCS overcrowding score only.
  • Read the Dashboard: All six model outputs update instantly. The central waterfall chart shows patient flow from total generated → virtual care deflected → on-site treatment → ambulance transports. Side panels show individual model outputs with 95% confidence intervals.
  • Interpret the Alert Strip: The colored bar below the header summarizes system status. Blue = normal operations. Amber = elevated strain with specific reasons listed. Red = critical system threat (only triggered by NEDOCS ≥140 or temperature ≥98°F).

What Each Model Answers

  • Arbon Regression: "How many total patient encounters should we expect?" — adapts to venue type, seating, environment, event type, and duration.
  • FIFA CWC 2025: "How many encounters and ambulance transports will a modern US stadium generate?" — calibrated to 63 real matches played in summer 2025.
  • Perron (Moderate Heat): "How does heat increase the patient rate?" — conservative estimate at 0.15 additional patients per 1,000 for every 10°F above the 85°F baseline.
  • Baird (Severe Heat): "How does extreme heat increase the patient rate?" — aggressive estimate at 0.30 additional patients per 1,000 per 10°F above baseline. Presented alongside Perron as a sensitivity range.
  • UVa Transport Model: "What level of medical resources should we stage?" — categorical output (Minor / Intermediate / Major) guiding whether to deploy EMS only, on-site physicians, or a field hospital.
  • NEDOCS: "How crowded is the receiving ED right now?" — universally validated hospital overcrowding metric. This measures the destination, not the event.

Reading the Waterfall Chart

The central waterfall shows how the total patient volume is reduced through interventions before reaching the hospital grid. The first bar is total patients generated (FIFA model). The second bar subtracts 25% for virtual care deflection (aspirational target based on Qatar 2022 data). The third bar subtracts patients treated and released on-site. The final bar shows the remaining ambulance transports that will hit the emergency department grid.

Locked Constants

Two values are locked and cannot be changed by users: the Baseline PPR (0.31 per 1,000 attendees, derived from the FIFA Club World Cup 2025 ground-truth data) and the Baseline Heat Index floor (85°F, the physiological threshold established in the literature). These protect the validated mathematical foundation from accidental modification.

Monte Carlo Simulation — 10,000 Iterations Per Model

Each model's point estimate is stress-tested by running it 10,000 times with randomized input variation: temperature ±3°F (SD), humidity ±5% (SD), attendance ±5% (SD). The resulting distributions generate 95% prediction intervals — the range within which the true value will fall 95% of the time given real-world input uncertainty. A tighter distribution means the prediction is more robust to weather forecast errors and attendance fluctuations.

Model Comparison — Arbon vs. FIFA

When configured for a bounded, seated, outdoor stadium, the Arbon model typically predicts 2–3× more patients than the FIFA CWC 2025 regression. This is expected, not a flaw. Arbon was derived from chaotic outdoor Australian events (festivals, marathons, community gatherings). The FIFA model was calibrated on 63 controlled US stadium matches. For modern stadium predictions, the FIFA model is more appropriate. Arbon serves as the conservative upper bound — useful for planning safety margins and for unbounded outdoor events like fan fests and watch parties where the crowd dynamics more closely match Arbon's original dataset.

Model Performance Metrics — Reference

Regression Metrics (Arbon, FIFA — Continuous Outputs)

These metrics measure how close the model's predicted patient count is to what actually happened.

R² (R-Squared)
Proportion of variance explained. If R² = 0.65, it means 65% of the medical surge volume is directly explained by the variables in the model (heat, crowd size, venue type). The remaining 35% is random chance or untracked factors.
Range: 0 to 1. Higher = better fit. R² > 0.5 is generally acceptable for mass gathering medicine.
MAE (Mean Absolute Error)
The average raw prediction error. If MAE = 12, the model's prediction is, on average, off by 12 patients. This is the most intuitive metric for hospital administrators and incident commanders.
Lower = more accurate. Units match the output (patients).
RMSE (Root Mean Square Error)
Similar to MAE but severely punishes large errors. If the model is usually accurate but completely fails to predict one massive heat-wave surge, RMSE will spike disproportionately. This is the "worst-case reliability" check.
Lower = more reliable. Always ≥ MAE. A large RMSE-to-MAE gap signals occasional catastrophic misses.
Feature Importance (FI)
Reveals which input variable is doing the most work in the equation. In summer mass gatherings, FI typically shows that Heat Index is significantly more important than crowd mood or event type.
Helps identify which variables matter most for operational planning.

Classification Metrics (UVa Model — Categorical Outputs)

These metrics measure how well the UVa model sorts events into the correct threat category (Minor / Intermediate / Major).

Precision
"When the model predicted a patient needed the ED, how often were they right?" High precision means you aren't wasting ED beds on minor injuries. Also called "Positive Predictive Value."
Range: 0 to 1. Higher = fewer false alarms.
Recall (Sensitivity)
"Of all patients who truly needed the ED, how many did the model catch?" High recall means you aren't accidentally leaving critical patients at the stadium first aid tent.
Range: 0 to 1. Higher = fewer missed critical patients. In disaster medicine, recall is prioritized over precision.
F1-Score
The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. Balances both concerns — false alarms and missed patients — into a single number. F1 > 0.7 is generally considered good performance.
Range: 0 to 1. Useful when you need one number to summarize classification quality.
AUC-ROC
Measures the model's ability to distinguish between threat levels. AUC = 0.5 is equivalent to a coin flip. AUC = 0.9 means the model reliably separates routine matches from mass casualty events.
Range: 0.5 to 1. Higher = better discrimination. AUC > 0.8 is considered strong.
⚠️ These metrics cannot be computed within this dashboard. They require the raw observed outcomes from the 63 FIFA Club World Cup 2025 matches — specifically, the actual patient counts and actual transport counts per match compared against each model's predictions. Once that dataset is available, the validation sequence is: (1) LOOCV on the FIFA regression to calculate its R², MAE, RMSE; (2) Historical back-testing of Arbon against the same 63 matches to quantify its overprediction; (3) Classification testing of UVa predictions vs. actual event severity to generate Precision, Recall, F1, and AUC.

Limitations

No External Validation. The FIFA CWC 2025 regression has not been validated on an independent dataset. Its coefficients were derived from and tested against the same 63 matches. LOOCV is planned but requires the raw match-level data. Until externally validated, projections should be treated as estimates, not guarantees.
Arbon Calibration Mismatch. The Arbon model was derived from Australian mass gatherings — outdoor festivals, marathons, and community events. Applying it to US stadium environments produces systematic overprediction (typically 2–3× above observed FIFA CWC 2025 rates). It is retained as a conservative upper bound, not a primary predictor for modern stadiums.
Linear Heat Assumption. Both the Baird and Perron heat coefficients model heat-related patient increases as linear functions. Physiologically, heat illness follows an exponential curve at extreme temperatures. Above approximately 105°F Heat Index, these models may significantly underpredict heat-stroke casualties. A nonlinear (exponential or logistic) heat function would improve accuracy at extreme temperatures but requires additional calibration data.
Subjective UVa Inputs. The UVa model depends on operator assessments of "Crowd Mood" and "Alcohol Availability" — inherently subjective judgments that can vary between users. This limits inter-rater reliability. The model's categorical output (Minor/Intermediate/Major) should be treated as a staging guide, not a precise risk quantification.
NEDOCS Is Pre-Surge Only. The NEDOCS score reflects the current baseline state of the receiving ED — it does not dynamically update based on the surge volume generated by the event models. To estimate post-surge ED impact, the user must mentally add the projected transport volume to their current census. A dynamic NEDOCS recalculation incorporating projected transports is a planned future feature.
No ICU or Inpatient Model. This dashboard does not project ICU admissions, inpatient bed consumption, or sustained multi-day hospital burden. For events generating critical patients (heat stroke, crush injuries, cardiac events), the downstream ICU impact may be operationally significant but is not modeled here.
Telemedicine Deflection Is Aspirational. The 25% virtual care deflection target is based on observational data from Qatar 2022 where infrastructure, cultural context, and healthcare system structure differ substantially from US host cities. Actual deflection rates will depend on local telehealth infrastructure, patient acuity mix, and regulatory environment. This figure should be used for planning scenarios, not as a guaranteed outcome.
Single-Event, Single-Venue Scope. This dashboard models one event at one venue at one point in time. The 2026 World Cup will involve simultaneous matches, overlapping fan fests, and multi-day cumulative strain across 16 host cities. Regional healthcare grid effects from concurrent events are not captured. Multi-venue, multi-day surge modeling is a critical area for future development.
Monte Carlo Assumes Normal Distributions. The 10,000-iteration Monte Carlo simulation varies temperature, humidity, and attendance using normal (Gaussian) distributions. In reality, weather events can produce bimodal or heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., a sudden thunderstorm drastically dropping temperature, or a record heat wave). The confidence intervals are therefore approximations, not exact probability bounds.
